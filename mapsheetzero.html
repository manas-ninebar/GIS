The situation

Everything below is already true. None of it is the product.

An RF engineer keeps a mobile network working. Every day they get a pile of evidence about invisible radio waves — cells and their sector antennas, signal-strength measurements crowdsourced from phones, drive tests, dropped-call counters, customer complaints, alarms — and every single piece of it has a latitude and longitude attached. The map is not a view onto their work. It is their work.

We have the data feeds, a clean pipeline, and a scoped list of twenty-one layers to draw. That part is engineering and it is going fine. What we do not have is a reason for anyone to love it. Every incumbent tool in this market can also draw dots on a map, and RF engineers already have three of them open.

The thing we can build that they cannot is a map that explains — one wired to an AI agent that investigates network faults, so the map is not just where you look, it is where the answer arrives.

Nobody has ever loved a list of checkboxes. Design the instrument, not the catalogue.

The four questions

Every map in this market answers two of these. Take the other two.

An engineer arrives at the map with a question. There are only ever four.

Solved everywhere

What is broken?

Which cells are underperforming today. A ranked list with colour on it. Table stakes.

Solved everywhere

Where is it?

Put the broken thing on a map so its neighbours and its geography are visible. Also table stakes.

Wide open

Why is it broken?

A hill. A new building. An antenna pointing three degrees wrong. A neighbour that went down at 02:00. Geography is the explanation — and no tool draws the explanation, only the symptom.

Wide open

Did it get better?

Before and after. The single question a new cell site exists to answer, and the one every tool makes you answer by opening two windows and squinting.

Ten provocations

Open problems. Pick one and go strange with it.

These are questions, not requirements. Each has a handle so you can say "I took P4." Disagreeing with a provocation, with evidence, is a valid answer.

P1

Time is the second map

Every layer has a clock — measurements, alarms, complaints, counters. Right now time is one slider bolted to one layer.

What single control lets you scrub, compare two moments, and play a sequence — without turning into a video editor?
P2

Before / after is the whole product

A new site is judged entirely by what changed after it switched on. Everyone reaches for a swipe divider. Everyone is probably wrong.

Is the answer two panes, a swipe, a blink, or a third layer that renders only the difference and nothing else?
P3

Thirty wedges on one dot

An urban rooftop carries three sectors × five frequency bands × two network generations at identical coordinates. Draw them all and you get an orange blob. Draw one and you are lying.

How does a single site render honestly at country zoom, city zoom, and street zoom — as three different things, or one thing that grows up?
P4

Colour is a scarce resource

Five layers each colour-coded by value produce mud. But engineers genuinely need to see signal quality and call drops and alarms together.

What is the rule that keeps exactly one thing loud and everything else structural — and what happens when a user insists on breaking it?
P5

It should open on an answer

Today it opens on a blank map of Japan and waits. An engineer responsible for forty sites has to work the tool before the tool works for them.

What is "today's map" for one person? Is it even a map, or a set of small ones you tap into?
P6

The map is also a graph

Behind the map is a knowledge graph: this cell relates to that cell, this alarm touched these counters, this rule fired on this evidence. Geography and topology are two projections of one truth.

Two linked views, or one thing that morphs between space and network? Show us the transition.
P7

Let the agent author the map

Our AI agent writes fault investigations. Imagine it can also emit a saved map — layers, filters, time window, camera position, legend — as its evidence.

What does it feel like to receive a map from an agent? Can you step through its reasoning as camera moves? Can you edit it and send it back?
P8

A map that admits doubt

Some coordinates are surveyed. Some are guessed from a scribbled address. A confidently misplaced pin surrounded by real data looks exactly like a right answer — this is the documented way AI mapping fails.

How do you render uncertainty so it is impossible to miss and doesn't wreck the picture?
P9

3D has to earn its place

Terrain and buildings genuinely explain coverage gaps — radio does not go through hills. Japan publishes free national elevation data and a free 3D model of two hundred cities. But 3D is slower, heavier, and harder to read than a flat map.

When does the third dimension switch on — and does the user ask for it, or does the map decide?
P10

The paste-into-chat problem

How work actually spreads in an operations team: someone screenshots their screen and drops it in a thread. A screenshot loses the layers, the legend, the time, and the truth.

One action, one link, exactly what they were looking at — and still correct when opened next week. What is that object?
Reference shelf

Ten places we looked, and the one idea worth taking from each.

Go look at all of them. Take the idea, not the interface — most of these are ugly.

Foundations

Four things we think must exist underneath, whatever you design on top.

Argue with these too — but if you replace one, replace it with something that does the same job.

Clock

One time control, shared

Not per-layer. Every layer registers against a single clock that can hold one moment or two.

Selection

One selection, everywhere

Pick a cell — on the map, in a table, in a chart — and every other surface responds. This is what makes a tool feel like one tool.

Recipe

A map you can name and send

Layers + filters + time + camera as one saveable object. Users save them. Agents emit them. Links carry them.

Provenance

Every shape knows its source

Where it came from, how fresh it is, how confident. Visible on screen, not buried in a payload.

Rules of the game

Six constraints. They are the interesting part.

  • Read-onlyThe map never changes the live network. Anything actionable leaves through a separate approval path. Design within that — a preview of a change is not a change.
  • No fake precisionSix decimal places on a guessed coordinate is a lie told fluently. If we can't say where a shape came from, we don't draw it.
  • Experts, not novicesOur users know these tools better than we do. Don't explain a map to them. Give them faster verbs and get out of the way.
  • Speed is a featureToggle to painted in under a tenth of a second, or it feels broken. Budget it before you design it.
  • Japanese network, Japanese dataKanji labels, national elevation and 3D city data, dense urban geometry. Design for Tokyo, not for a demo city.
  • Beautiful is not optionalThis gets shown to people who have stared at the same grey engineering software for fifteen years. Looking extraordinary is part of the argument, not decoration on top of it.
What good looks like

Three tests. All three are about other people, not about us.

The five-second test

An RF engineer glances at it and says "that's an RF tool" — not "that's a map with your data on it." Usually decided by how you draw a single site.

The morning test

They open it before their email. Which means it has to already know what happened overnight and have an opinion about it.

The thread test

They send a colleague a link to it instead of a screenshot — and the colleague sees the same thing, still true, next week.

Field vocabulary

Ten words, so the domain stops being a wall.

You do not need radio engineering to design this. You do need these ten.

Cell
One antenna's worth of coverage. A site has many.
Sector
The wedge of ground a cell points at. Usually three per site.
Azimuth
The compass direction a sector faces.
Tilt
How far down the antenna aims. The main knob for fixing coverage.
RSRP
Signal strength, in dBm. Higher is better; −105 is roughly the pain threshold.
SINR
Signal quality — signal versus everything interfering with it.
Handover
Passing a moving phone from one cell to the next. When it fails, calls drop.
Neighbour
A cell close enough to hand over to. Chosen geographically — by which sectors face you.
Coverage hole
An area with no usable signal. The thing new sites are built to close.
Drive test
Someone drives a route with measuring equipment. Ground truth, one road at a time.
Bring us

Ideas, in whatever form makes them convincing.

  1. One provocation, taken seriously. Pick from P1–P10. Depth beats coverage — we would rather see one idea pushed to the point of being uncomfortable than ten sketched politely.
  2. Show it, don't describe it. A rough clickable thing beats a beautiful static one. A beautiful static one beats a paragraph. Fake data is completely fine.
  3. Tell us what you'd cut. Twenty-one layers is too many. Which eight would you actually ship, and what does the map become when the other thirteen are gone?
  4. Bring one reference we haven't seen. From anywhere — a game, a flight tracker, an audio tool, a weather app, a museum piece. The best map ideas rarely come from maps.
